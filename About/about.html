About this Space



Across three interactive pages, users experience the false choice and authorship, growing aware of how their contributions are consumed by the system (corporate control of the web). My project brings to light the power imbalance between users and the systems that claim to care for us online. It asks viewers to consider: What it means for online users to create or be seen when the system defines the frame?

How Should Users Interact?

This first webpage allows users to interact with a simulated instagram post, choosing whether or not to share their artwork (a silkscreen). Nevertheless, the system shares and copies their content, making users feel their choice is unimportant. This emphasises how users should be careful about their contributions online under a system. This reveals how platforms/systems can reproduce infinitely and profit from user submissions. The user is invited to play a game as a call to action to “save” the artworks (already reproduced by systems) by catching/clicking on the silkscreens falling, restoring them to original versions. After the game, users are shown that all silkscreens were consumed by the “machine” below, showing that their efforts were futile.

The second page references locusts from the biblical Ten Plagues of Egypt, alluding to the environmental harm systems can have. The data centers of the system need resources like rare minerals to power itself, akin to how locusts devour the land. When users read a line from the bible, the system rewrites it. When users choose the destiny of the locusts, the system shows both choices have the same result. This reinforces the concept of users’ loss of control.

The third page features a centered cyanotype where users can customize with flowers/plants. The white, disappearing flowers surrounded by robotic imagery (that alludes to the system) elicit reflection from users on the environmental damages and authorship. A button below leads users to the last page with a prompt question about who owns the cyanotype. Meanwhile, it makes users choose from default options, underscoring how systems shape our choices. In a Canva link, all viewers are invited to answer a question about their thoughts on how technology shapes the meaning of art.

Moreover, the religious metaphors embedded in cathedral silkscreens and locusts page, is intended for further exploration about how users can be under both the surveillance of religion and the system (or techonology). But both watches and shapes us, for good or for ill. This parallel fosters user reflection about what is promised and taken when systems asserts to care for us. This concept is inspired by Chia Amisola’s “Cathedral” video performance.
